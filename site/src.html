The fine print

What this is, and what it cannot tell you.

What is this, and whose data is it?

Olist is a Brazilian company that lets small merchants sell on the country’s large shopping sites through one shared storefront, and it arranges the delivery. In 2018 it published the records of about 100,000 of its orders. I wrote this the way I would for its leadership team at the start of September 2018, which is where the data ends. I have never worked for Olist, so everything here comes from that public file. Amounts are in Brazilian reais, and one real was worth about 27 US cents in 2018.

What the measures mean
Sales
The price of the goods in an order, without the delivery charge, for every order that was not cancelled or found to be out of stock.
Active seller
A seller who made at least one sale in the month.
Typical delivery time
The middle value, so that half of orders arrived sooner and half later, counted from purchase to delivery.
Late
Delivered on a later day than the date the customer was shown at checkout.
Bad review
A rating of one or two stars out of five. Where an order was rated twice, I kept the more recent rating.
What the data cannot tell us
  • The file may not hold every order. Olist did not say how the published orders were chosen. I have treated the file as the whole business. If orders were left out, the totals here are too low and repeat purchases are undercounted. Rates measured inside each order, such as the share that arrived late, would not be affected.
  • Nothing is recorded before a purchase. Visits, searches, competitors’ prices and advertising are all missing, which is why the review can show that demand fell and cannot explain it.
  • There is one delivery date for each order, so the records cannot show when each parcel of an order arrived.
  • These are patterns and not experiments. Where it matters I have proposed a test.
  • The data is old. It ends in 2018, and this review does not describe Olist as it is today.
How this was built

The public file is nine linked tables of orders, items, payments, ratings, customers, sellers and products. I loaded them into a database, wrote SQL to build one table with a row for each order, and a Python script computes every figure on this page from that table. The page is filled in from the script’s results when it is built, so no number here was typed by hand. {{code_sentence}}
