Business review · Olist marketplace · January 2017 to August 2018

More sellers, same sales

Olist’s sales more than tripled during 2017, and they have not grown since last November. This review explains why signing up more sellers stopped lifting sales, shows where orders still go wrong, and sets out what I would do in the twelve weeks before Black Friday.

  • Prepared by Timur Kherlenbayar
  • Data through 31 August 2018
  • The summary takes two minutes to read

About this report. Olist is a Brazilian company that lets small merchants sell on the country’s large shopping sites through one shared storefront, and it arranges the delivery for them. In 2018 it published the records of about 100,000 of its orders so that anyone could study them. I have written this review the way I would write it for the company’s leadership team at the start of September 2018, which is where the data ends. I have never worked for Olist, so everything here comes from that public file, and the notes at the end say what the file cannot tell us. Amounts are in Brazilian reais, and one real was worth about 27 US cents in 2018.

The two-minute version

Executive summary

The marketplace is running better than it ever has, and it is selling less than it did in the spring. Over the last three months a typical order arrived in 7 days and only 3.6% of orders came late, which are the best figures on record. Monthly sales, at R$863 thousand, are nevertheless 13% below the three months before, and no month has matched last November. The number of sellers keeps rising, so each seller now sells about a quarter less than at the start of the year. Signing up more sellers is no longer what grows this business.

Each figure is the average for June to August 2018. The small lines show all twenty months, with the latest three in blue. “Bad reviews” are ratings of one or two stars out of five.

1Growth

Sales more than tripled in 2017 and have not grown since November.

In the first three months of 2017 Olist sold about R$244 thousand of goods a month. By the last three months of that year it was selling R$802 thousand a month, which is 3.3 times as much, and November reached R$1.0 million with the help of Black Friday. That is still the record. Sales held near R$950 thousand a month from January to May of this year and then slipped to R$863 thousand a month across June, July and August. August was still 49% ahead of August 2017, but all of that growth had been won before this year began.

The number of sellers kept growing, and sales per seller did not.

A marketplace can grow in two ways. It can have more sellers, or each seller can sell more, so it helps to split sales into those two parts. Olist had 968 active sellers in January and 1,266 in August, and about 158 new sellers made their first sale in each month of this year. Over the same months the amount an average seller sold fell from R$977 to R$671.

It would be easy to explain this away by saying that new sellers start small and pull the average down. That is not what happened. I followed the 1,238 sellers who sold both in January to May and in June to August. Together they sold R$862 thousand a month in the first period and R$733 thousand a month in the second, which is a drop of 15%. New sellers added R$130 thousand a month. That more than covered the R$89 thousand lost with the sellers who stopped, but it could not also cover the decline among the sellers who stayed.

What this means

When established sellers with the same catalogues sell 15% less, the likeliest reason is that fewer shoppers are reaching them or buying from them. Recruiting more sellers then divides the same demand among more shops. The decline was also uneven. Computer accessories fell by 45% and sports and leisure by 33%, while health and beauty grew by 28%, and the scorecard at the end shows every large category.

I cannot tell from the order file why demand softened, because it holds no record of visits, of competitors’ prices or of advertising. Some of it may be the season, since there is only one earlier year to compare with, and June and July followed a national truckers’ strike and included a World Cup. That uncertainty is the reason for my third recommendation.

2Customers

Almost every order comes from someone buying for the first time.

Of the 94,707 customers who bought during these twenty months, 97% bought exactly once. This year only 3.5% of orders came from someone who had bought before. How well the first order went makes some difference to whether a customer returns, but not much.

What this means

Two decisions follow from this. The first is that a loyalty programme is unlikely to pay for itself soon, because even delighted customers rarely return. Shoppers buy on the large shopping sites and many of them probably never learn that Olist was involved. The second is that the business has to win nearly every order afresh, which makes the reputation of the shared storefront worth more than any one customer’s goodwill. The next two sections look at what damages that reputation.

3Bad orders

One order in seven ends in a bad review, and four kinds of order produce most of them.

After every order the customer is asked to rate it from one to five stars. Across the period 14.6% of rated orders received one or two stars, and I will call that a bad review. An ordinary order, meaning a single item that arrived on time, gets a bad review 7.5% of the time. Four kinds of order do far worse. They make up only 18% of orders and they account for 58% of the bad reviews.

Orders that never arrive are already becoming rarer.

Customers paid R$570 thousand, which is 3.6% of all payments, for 2,889 orders that never reached them. Some were cancelled, some turned out to be out of stock after the customer had paid, and some were shipped and never recorded as delivered. This is the one problem in the report that has largely been dealt with. These orders were 3.7% of the total in 2017 and 1.9% in the last three months, mostly because out-of-stock sales fell from 1.0% of orders to 0.2%. I would keep the figure on the monthly dashboard and would not start a new project for it.

Orders with more than one item are judged as a whole, and often only part has come.

About 9% of orders contain more than one item. Even when the order was recorded as delivered on time, 21% of those from a single seller received a bad review, and so did 47% of those that drew on several sellers. The written comments say why. I searched the comments on these bad reviews for Portuguese phrases such as “só recebi um” and “faltou”, which mean “I only received one” and “something was missing”. They appear in 53% of the comments on orders with several items, and in 9% of the comments on single-item orders.

So the system marks the order as delivered when the first parcel lands, the customer is asked for one rating for the whole order, and the answer is given while half of it is still missing. Fixing that is my fourth recommendation.

4Delivery

Delivery is faster than ever. The danger is a handful of bad weeks, and Black Friday is twelve weeks away.

A typical order now arrives in 7 days, compared with 13 days in the first quarter of this year, and 3.6% of orders arrive after the date the customer was promised. Lateness in this business is not a steady drip. It comes in episodes. November and December 2017 and February and March 2018 held 27% of the orders and 55% of the late deliveries. In the other sixteen months 4.2% of orders were late, and in those four months 13.5% were.

Rio de Janeiro took the worst of it. The state receives 13% of orders and 23% of late deliveries, and during the four bad months 30% of the parcels sent there arrived late.

Customers forgive a slow delivery and punish a broken promise.

Olist gives every customer a delivery date at checkout. On average it promises 24 days and delivers in 13, and that cushion matters, because the rating follows the promise much more closely than it follows the speed. Single-item orders that took three to four weeks and still arrived by the promised date averaged 4.0 stars. Orders that arrived four to seven days after the promised date averaged 2.1.

Three late customers in four are asked for a rating while they are still waiting.

Olist’s description of its data says that the rating request goes out when the order is delivered or when the promised date passes, whichever comes first. The file bears that out. For 74% of the late orders, the request was sent before the parcel arrived. Among orders that were one to three days late, customers who were asked after the parcel came gave 3.5 stars on average, and customers who were asked while still waiting gave 2.4.

Holding the request until the parcel arrives, and sending an apology with a new date in its place, is the cheapest change in this report. It would not make a single parcel faster, and part of the gain would be a fairer measurement and not a happier customer. It would still stop the business from collecting its worst reviews at the worst possible moment.

Most late parcels were handed over on time by the seller.

In 72% of late orders the seller met its shipping deadline, so the time was lost with the carrier. A late parcel spent a typical 26 days in the carrier’s hands, against 7 days for one that arrived on time. Sellers who shipped late account for the other 28%, and they are concentrated. Just 81 sellers, 3% of those who shipped anything, are responsible for half of all the missed shipping deadlines.

Something I tested and dropped

I checked whether Olist could do better with a simple rule for the promised date, which was to promise each state the time within which 95% of its deliveries had arrived over the previous four weeks. Run across the whole period, the rule made 6.8% of orders late against the 7.0% that really were, and it did so by promising 2 more days on average. The existing promise is already about as good as recent history allows, because the bad weeks give no warning in the data. That is why my recommendation is to plan for the peaks we know are coming and to tell customers when a parcel is late, and not to adopt a new formula.

5Sellers

A few dozen sellers carry the business, and a third of new sellers stop within months.

Olist is paid by its sellers, so they are its real customers, and the seller base has two weaknesses. The first is how much rests on a few of them. Over the twenty months 3,029 sellers made at least one sale. The top one percent, which is 31 sellers, brought in 26% of all sales, and the top five percent brought in 54%. The smaller half of the base, 1,500 sellers, brought in 3%.

The second weakness is that many sellers do not last. Of the 2,023 sellers who made their first sale before March 2018, 35% made no sale at all in their fourth, fifth and sixth months. The sellers who joined during 2017 show what that costs. Together they sold R$742 thousand a month between January and May and R$479 thousand a month between June and August, which is 35% less, and the number of them still selling fell from 1,057 to 777.

The first month shows who will stay.

How a seller’s first thirty days go is a strong sign of what follows. Sellers who received a single order in that time, who are 39% of all new sellers, had a 47% chance of still selling in months four to six. Sellers who received ten or more had a 94% chance, and they went on to sell about 17 times as much each month.

What this means

This is a pattern and not proof. Sellers with better products get more orders early and also last longer, so helping a weak seller to four orders may not turn it into a strong one. It is still the earliest signal the business has, it arrives within a month, and it gives the team that looks after sellers something concrete to aim at. I would test it on one intake of new sellers before changing anyone’s targets, and my second recommendation describes how.

6Recommendations

Four things to do, in the order I would do them.

For each one I have set out the first step, what the evidence suggests it is worth, how we would know it had worked, and how sure I am. One limit applies to all four. The order file lets me count late parcels and bad reviews with confidence, and it does not let me turn a bad review into lost reais, so I have given the size of each problem in orders and reviews and have used money only where the file records it.

1 · Before 23 November

Prepare for Black Friday now.

Last year’s peak and the disruption that followed produced more than half of all the late deliveries in this report. The dates of this year’s peak are known, and so is the place where it will hurt most. For orders placed in the weeks around Black Friday and Christmas, promise longer delivery times to Rio de Janeiro and the Northeast, and reserve extra carrier capacity on the route from São Paulo to Rio. Change the rating request so that it waits for the parcel, and send an apology with a new date when the promised day passes.

First step
Agree the peak calendar and the longer promise with the carriers this month, and change the timing of the rating request.
What it is worth
If the four bad months had run at the ordinary late rate, about 2,500 orders would have arrived on time and about 1,300 bad reviews would not have been written.
How we will know
The share of orders that arrive late, by week and by destination, stays under 5% for Rio through November and December.
Confidence
High on the diagnosis, and less on how much of the delay Olist can control.

2 · This quarter

Count the sellers who sell.

Adding sellers has stopped adding sales, and a third of new sellers fade within months. I would replace the target for the number of sellers signed with a target for the share of new sellers who reach four orders in their first thirty days, which 29% of them do today. Give each new seller help with listings and prices during that month. Separately, give the 155 sellers who bring in 54% of sales a named person to call, because losing a few of them would cost more than a bad month.

First step
Take next month’s new sellers, give half of them the extra help, and compare the two halves after ninety days.
What it is worth
Sellers who joined in 2017 now sell R$263 thousand a month less than they did in the spring. Winning back a tenth of that is worth about R$26 thousand a month.
How we will know
The share of new sellers reaching four orders in thirty days rises, and sales per active seller stop falling.
Confidence
Medium. The pattern is strong, and the test is what would show that help causes sellers to stay.

3 · Before next year’s plan

Find out where the buyers went.

The most important number in this report is that sellers who were active all year are selling 15% less than they were in the spring. I can show that it happened and I cannot show why, because the order file starts at the moment of purchase. The answer is in data the company holds and did not publish, which is the number of visits to listings, the share of visits that end in an order, and Olist’s prices against competing listings, each by shopping site and by category. I would start with computer accessories and sports and leisure, where the falls were largest.

First step
Pull visits and orders by shopping site and category for January to August, and see whether the fall is in visits or in conversion.
What it is worth
The gap between spring and summer is R$87 thousand a month, or about R$1.0 million over a year if it persists.
How we will know
Leadership can say which of three things happened: fewer visits, lower conversion, or a seasonal dip that 2019 will repeat.
Confidence
High that this is the right question. The answer is unknown.

4 · Next quarter

Repair orders that hold more than one item.

One order in eleven holds more than one item, and those orders are rated badly three times as often as ordinary ones even when the records say they arrived on time. Half of the complaints say that only part of the order came. I would track each parcel of an order separately, show the customer a date for each one, mark the order as delivered only when the last parcel arrives, and send the rating request after that.

First step
Check whether parcel-level tracking already exists with the carriers, and read a hundred of these complaints with the support team.
What it is worth
9,008 orders over twenty months, and about 1,500 bad reviews beyond what ordinary orders would have received.
How we will know
The share of bad reviews on multi-item orders falls from 25% towards the 7.5% of ordinary orders.
Confidence
Medium. The comments are clear about the cause, and the file cannot confirm when each parcel arrived.

Three things I considered and would not do.

  • A loyalty programme for shoppers.

    About 4 customers in a hundred come back, and a perfect first order moves that figure by less than a point. Money spent here would buy very little.

  • A new formula for the promised delivery date.

    The simple version I tested did no better than the promise Olist already makes, as section 4 explains.

  • A project on out-of-stock orders.

    They have already fallen from 1.0% of orders to 0.2%, so it is enough to keep watching the number.

What the words mean, what the data cannot tell us, and how this was built.

What the measures mean

Sales
The price of the goods in an order, without the delivery charge, for every order that was not cancelled or found to be out of stock. Olist’s own income is a commission and a fee taken from these sales, and the file does not record it.
Active seller
A seller who made at least one sale in the month.
Typical delivery time
The middle value, so that half of orders arrived sooner and half later, counted from the moment of purchase to the moment of delivery.
Late
Delivered on a later calendar day than the date the customer was shown at checkout.
Bad review
A rating of one or two stars out of five. Where an order was rated twice, I kept the more recent rating.

What the data cannot tell us

  • The file may not hold every order. Olist published about 100,000 orders and did not say how they were chosen. I have treated the file as if it were the whole business. If orders were left out, the totals here are too low and repeat purchases are undercounted. Rates that are measured inside each order, such as the share that arrived late and the ratings that late orders received, would not be affected.
  • The first and last months are incomplete. The file holds a few hundred orders from 2016 and stops partway through September 2018, so the review covers January 2017 to August 2018.
  • There is no record of what happened before a purchase. Visits, searches, prices on competing listings and advertising are all missing, which is why the review can show that demand per seller fell and cannot explain it.
  • There is one delivery date for each order. For an order that came in several parcels, the file cannot show when each one arrived.
  • These are patterns and not experiments. Late orders get worse ratings, and busy new sellers stay longer, but the file cannot prove that changing one would change the other. Where it matters I have proposed a test.
  • The data is old. It ends in 2018. I chose it because it is a rare public record of a real company’s orders, sellers, deliveries and ratings together, and this review does not describe Olist as it is today.

How this was built

The public file is nine linked tables of orders, items, payments, ratings, customers, sellers and products. I loaded them into DuckDB and wrote SQL to build one table with a row for each order, and a Python script then computes every figure in this report from that table. The page is filled in from the script’s results when it is built, so no number here was typed by hand. The SQL and the scripts are on GitHub.

The data is the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, which the company shares under a Creative Commons licence for non-commercial use (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).
