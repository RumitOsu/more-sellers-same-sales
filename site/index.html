A business review · Olist marketplace, Brazil · 2017 to 2018

More sellers, same sales

A marketplace kept signing up sellers and stopped growing anyway. This is the review I would hand its leadership team: what happened, why, and what to do before Black Friday.

The 60-second version ↓ Test your instinct first
What is this, and whose data is it?

Olist is a Brazilian company that lets small merchants sell on the country’s large shopping sites through one shared storefront, and it arranges the delivery. In 2018 it published the records of about 100,000 of its orders. I wrote this the way I would for its leadership team at the start of September 2018, which is where the data ends. I have never worked for Olist, so everything here comes from that public file, and the notes at the end say what it cannot tell us. Amounts are in Brazilian reais, and one real was worth about 27 US cents in 2018.

The 60-second version

Running better than ever, and selling less than in spring.

Orders now arrive in 7 days and only 3.6% come late, the best figures on record. Yet monthly sales are 13% lower than in the spring, and signing up more sellers has stopped helping, because each seller now sells about a quarter less.

Before the evidence, a quick test

Olist kept adding sellers all through 2018. Draw what you think sales did.

Sales more than tripled during 2017, and about 158 new sellers made their first sale in every month of 2018. Drag across the shaded area to finish the line.

1Growth

Sales peaked in November 2017 and never got back there.

Sales went from R$244 thousand a month in early 2017 to R$1.0 million in November, with the help of Black Friday. This year they held near R$950 thousand a month until May and then slipped to R$863 thousand over the summer.

Sales are the number of sellers multiplied by what each one sells. One went up and the other went down.

New sellers starting small is not the explanation. The 1,238 sellers who sold in both spring and summer sold 15% less. Newcomers more than replaced the sellers who left, but they could not cover that drop as well.

What this means

When the same sellers with the same products sell 15% less, fewer shoppers are finding them or buying from them. Adding sellers then only splits the same demand more ways.

The order records begin at the moment of purchase, so they cannot say why demand fell. Part of it may be the season, with a truckers’ strike in May and a World Cup in June and July. Finding out is my third recommendation.

2Customers

Nearly every order comes from a first-time buyer.

97% of customers bought exactly once, and even a perfect first order barely changes that.

What this means

A loyalty programme would buy very little here. Shoppers buy on the big shopping sites and may never learn that Olist was involved. So the business has to win almost every order from scratch, and the reputation of its shared storefront is what it most needs to protect.

3Bad orders

One order in seven ends in a bad review, and most of them trace to a few kinds of order.

An ordinary order, meaning one item that arrives on time, gets a bad review 7.5% of the time. Orders that go wrong get them far more often.

3.7% → 1.9%

Orders that never arrive are getting rarer. Customers paid R$570 thousand for 2,889 orders that never reached them, but the share has halved since 2017. It needs watching and not a new project.

53%

of complaints on multi-item orders say only part of it came. On single-item orders it is 9%. The order is marked as delivered when the first parcel lands, and the customer rates all of it while half is still missing.

4Delivery

Delivery is faster than ever. A few bad weeks do the damage, and Black Friday is twelve weeks away.

A typical order now takes 7 days, down from 13 at the start of the year. Lateness comes in bursts. Four months held 27% of the orders and 55% of the late deliveries, and Rio de Janeiro took the worst of it.

Customers forgive slow. They do not forgive late.

Olist promises a delivery date at checkout, on average 24 days out, and usually beats it. The rating follows that promise far more than it follows speed. An order that takes three to four weeks and still arrives when promised averages 4.0 stars.

74%

of late customers were asked for a rating before the parcel arrived. Among orders one to three days late, people asked while still waiting gave 2.4 stars, and people asked afterwards gave 3.5.

72%

of late parcels left the seller on time. The days were lost with the carrier, where a late parcel spent a typical 26 days against 7 for one that arrived on time.

81

sellers cause half of all missed shipping deadlines. That is 3% of the sellers who ship anything, so a short list of calls would cover most of the problem.

Something I tested and dropped

I checked whether a simple rule could set a better promised date, by promising each state the time within which 95% of its deliveries had arrived over the previous four weeks. Across the whole period the rule made 6.8% of orders late against the 7.0% that really were, and it only managed that by promising 2 more days. The promise Olist already makes is about as good as recent history allows, because the bad weeks give no warning. That is why I recommend planning for the peaks we know are coming, and not a new formula.

5Sellers

A few dozen sellers carry the business, and one new seller in three fades within months.

Olist is paid by its sellers, so they are its real customers. Of 3,029 sellers, the top 31 bring in 26% of sales, and the smaller half bring in 3%.

Many sellers do not last. 35% of new sellers made no sale at all in their fourth to sixth months. A seller’s first month is the best early sign of who will stay.

What this means

This is a pattern and not proof, because good products win early orders and also last longer. It is still the earliest signal there is, and it gives the team that looks after sellers a clear target. I would test it on one month’s new sellers before changing anyone’s goals.

6Recommendations

Four things to do, in the order I would do them.

Try it yourself

What would fixing these be worth?

Drag the sliders and watch the share of bad reviews move. One honest limit: the records let me count bad reviews, and they do not let me turn one into lost money, so the result is measured in unhappy customers.

1 · Before 23 November

Prepare for Black Friday now.

Last year’s peak and the disruption after it produced more than half of all late deliveries. This year’s dates are known. For orders placed around Black Friday and Christmas, promise longer delivery to Rio de Janeiro and the Northeast, book extra carrier space from São Paulo to Rio, and hold the rating request until the parcel has arrived.

First step
Agree the peak calendar and the longer promise with the carriers this month.
What it is worth
About 2,500 on-time orders and 1,300 fewer bad reviews, if the four bad months had run like ordinary ones.
How we will know
Late deliveries to Rio stay under 5% through November and December.
Confidence
High on the cause, less on how much of it Olist controls.

2 · This quarter

Count the sellers who sell.

Replace the target for sellers signed with one for new sellers who reach four orders in their first thirty days. Today 29% do, and 87% of them are still selling months later, against 57% of the rest. Separately, give the 155 sellers who bring in 54% of sales a named person to call.

First step
Give half of next month’s new sellers extra help with listings and prices, and compare the two halves after ninety days.
What it is worth
Sellers who joined in 2017 now sell R$263 thousand a month less than in spring. A tenth of that is R$26 thousand a month.
How we will know
More new sellers reach four orders, and sales per seller stop falling.
Confidence
Medium. The test is what would show that help makes sellers stay.

3 · Before next year’s plan

Find out where the buyers went.

The most important number here is that the same sellers sold 15% less in summer than in spring. I can show that it happened and not why. The answer is in data the company holds and did not publish: visits to listings, the share of visits that end in an order, and prices against competing listings, for each shopping site and category.

First step
Start with computer accessories and sports and leisure, where sales fell most.
What it is worth
The gap is R$87 thousand a month, or about R$1.0 million over a year if it lasts.
How we will know
Leadership can say whether it was fewer visits, fewer visits turning into orders, or a seasonal dip.
Confidence
High that this is the right question. The answer is unknown.

4 · Next quarter

Fix orders with more than one item.

One order in eleven holds more than one item, and those orders are rated badly three times as often as ordinary ones. Track each parcel separately, show the customer a date for each, and ask for the rating only after the last one arrives.

First step
Check whether the carriers already track each parcel, and read a hundred of these complaints with the support team.
What it is worth
About 1,500 bad reviews more than ordinary orders would have received, across 9,008 orders.
How we will know
Bad reviews on these orders fall from 25% towards 7.5%.
Confidence
Medium. The comments are clear, and the records cannot confirm when each parcel arrived.

Three things I considered and would not do.

A loyalty programme. About 4 customers in a hundred come back, and a perfect first order moves that by less than a point.

A new formula for the promised date. The simple version I tested did no better than the promise Olist already makes.

A project on out-of-stock orders. They have already fallen from 1.0% of orders to 0.2%.

Notes

The fine print.

What the measures mean
Sales
The price of the goods in an order, without the delivery charge, for every order that was not cancelled or found to be out of stock. Olist’s own income is a commission and a fee taken from these sales, and the records do not show it.
Active seller
A seller who made at least one sale in the month.
Typical delivery time
The middle value, so that half of orders arrived sooner and half later, counted from purchase to delivery.
Late
Delivered on a later day than the date the customer was shown at checkout.
Bad review
A rating of one or two stars out of five. Where an order was rated twice, I kept the more recent rating.
What the data cannot tell us
  • The file may not hold every order. Olist published about 100,000 orders and did not say how they were chosen. I have treated the file as the whole business. If orders were left out, the totals here are too low and repeat purchases are undercounted. Rates measured inside each order, such as the share that arrived late, would not be affected.
  • The first and last months are incomplete, so the review covers January 2017 to August 2018.
  • Nothing is recorded before a purchase. Visits, searches, competitors’ prices and advertising are all missing, which is why the review can show that demand fell and cannot explain it.
  • There is one delivery date for each order, so for an order that came in several parcels the records cannot show when each one arrived.
  • These are patterns and not experiments. Late orders get worse ratings and busy new sellers stay longer, but the records cannot prove that changing one would change the other. Where it matters I have proposed a test.
  • The data is old. It ends in 2018. I chose it because it is a rare public record of a real company’s orders, sellers, deliveries and ratings together, and this review does not describe Olist as it is today.
How this was built

The public file is nine linked tables of orders, items, payments, ratings, customers, sellers and products. I loaded them into a database, wrote SQL to build one table with a row for each order, and a Python script computes every figure in this report from that table. The page is filled in from the script’s results when it is built, so no number here was typed by hand. The SQL and the scripts are on GitHub.

The data is the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, which the company shares under a Creative Commons licence for non-commercial use (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).
